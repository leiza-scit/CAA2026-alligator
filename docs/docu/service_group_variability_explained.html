What one cell is

The unit of the figure, stated before anything is computed from it.

One cell of either panel is a pair: a chronological horizon (row) and a service group (column). Three groups are plotted — Assiette à bord oblique, Service I, Service II — against five horizons, so the grid has fifteen positions, of which twelve are occupied.

What the cell reports is not a date, and not a count. It is a measure of how far apart the chronological ranks of that group's sub-types lie, taken across all the material assigned to that horizon. The left panel gives that spread in rank units; the right panel gives the same spread rescaled to a bounded score. Nothing in either panel is calendar years.

Read the figure as one measurement shown twice. The right panel is a deterministic function of the left, evaluated cell by cell. A cell that is red on the left is necessarily low on the right. The two panels are not two lines of evidence and must never be cited as agreeing with one another.

From the spreadsheet to the cell

Four assignments, each of which can be argued with separately.

Where the five rows come from

The horizon assignment, and how far it follows the seriation.

The rows of the figure are not a mechanical cut of the seriation. FINDSPOT_HORIZON is a hand-maintained table — one line per findspot, edited when the archaeology says so — and the horizon intervals are then the envelope of the estimated dates of its members. Plotting the findspots on the first correspondence-analysis axis shows how closely the two agree, and exactly where they do not.

Findspot labels differ between the workbook and the TTL, so the lookup is not a plain dictionary hit: resolve_horizon() tries the label, then PERCENT_LABEL_CORRECTIONS, then that bridge in reverse. The camp the TTL calls Nijmegen, Lager appears in the workbook as Nijmegen, Lager (Haalebos), and is a different site from Nijmegen, Lager (Brunsting). Anything reading the horizon of a findspot must go through resolve_horizon() rather than index FINDSPOT_HORIZON directly, or the Haalebos camp silently drops out.

Why this figure belongs in the paper. It is the honest statement of what a horizon is here: an archaeological judgement informed by the seriation, not a quantile of it. Four departures out of forty-four is a good agreement and a much better claim than an unexamined one — and a reader who wants to test the variance result against a different grouping now knows precisely which four assignments to vary.

The forty-fifth findspot, and why it is not here

The correspondence analysis needs co-occurrence: a findspot earns a position on the axis from the company its types keep at other findspots. A findspot with a single type represented offers no such information — its row of the matrix has one mark — and it drops out. It therefore has no CA coordinate, and without one the Alligator cannot date it, so it reaches no horizon.

Exactly one findspot in this corpus is in that position. Trier-Petrisberg holds one oblique-rim plate and nothing else. The arithmetic closes on it:

The exclusion costs the figure nothing. The oblique-rim plate column is degenerate in every horizon — one sub-type, stage rank 1 — so the sherd would raise an \(n\) by one and leave \(s = 0.00\) and \(q = 1.00\) exactly where they are. Including it would mean assigning a horizon by hand to a findspot the seriation cannot place at all, which is a different kind of entry in FINDSPOT_HORIZON from a correction to a position the seriation did supply. The plot above would gain a point with no value on its own x-axis, and the claim that forty of forty-four fall in seriation order would stop being checkable.

Record the exclusion rather than the findspot. One sherd in 7131 is not a problem; an undocumented gap between the workbook total and the figure total is. A companion table to FINDSPOT_HORIZON — findspot, and the reason it carries no horizon — costs three lines, survives a rebuild, and can be asserted in the graph as provenance instead of living in a caption. The rule behind it belongs in the method section too: findspots with a single represented type are dropped by the correspondence analysis is a filter with a sample-size consequence, and a reviewer will ask.

The statistic as data

Why the ranks are properties of concepts rather than constants in a script.

Every quantity the figure rests on is a triple in a published graph: the sherd count, the stage rank, the group membership, the horizon. Nothing is a magic number inside the plotting code, and the consequence is that the figure can be recomputed by a reader who has never seen the script.

The sufficient statistics are the interoperability story. Because the within-group variance needs only \(N\), \(\sum r c\) and \(\sum r^{2} c\), the whole method survives a query engine that has neither SQRT nor EXP — which rdflib's SPARQL does not. The query returns three sums per cell; the printed figure, the browser figure and the notebook all take the last two steps in Python from those same three numbers, so none of the three can drift from the others.

The quantity that is dispersed

Ranks, not years — and a stage is not a horizon.

Two ordering systems meet in this figure and they are easily confused, because both run from early to late and both are numbered from 1.

A horizon is a bin of excavated contexts: Oberaden, Dangstetten and Basel sit in horizon 1 because the seriation puts them there. A stage is a position in the typological sequence of one service group: Service Ia, Ib, Ic. Nothing forces a horizon-1 assemblage to consist of stage-1 material, and in fact it does not — horizon 1 is already 55 % stage Ic. The figure exists precisely to measure that mixture.

The rank scale has no zero and no unit. Ranks are ordinal labels 1, 2, 3 … A standard deviation of them is defensible as a descriptive spread, but it is not a variance in years, and it changes if the ranks are renumbered — for example if an empty stage is dropped and the remaining ranks are closed up. The numbering used must travel with the figure.

Within-group variance

The left panel. compute_within_group_rgzm(), following the RGZM method.

Fix a horizon \(h\) and a service group \(g\). The observations are the sherds of \(g\) found at the findspots of \(h\); each one is valued by the stage rank of its sub-type. Write \(c_j\) for the sherd count of sub-type \(j\) and \(r_j\) for its rank.

\[ N = \sum_j c_j \qquad \overline{x} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_j r_j c_j \qquad s_{g,h} = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_j r_j^{2}c_j - N\overline{x}^{2}}{N-1}} \]
The sample standard deviation — STDDEV_SAMP, denominator \(N-1\). This is the value printed and coloured in the left panel. It is in rank units: \(s = 0\) means every sherd sits on one rank, and for a three-stage group the largest attainable value is about 1.0, reached when the mass splits evenly between ranks 1 and 3. Against that ceiling, horizon 1's 0.68 is a genuinely mixed assemblage and horizon 5's 0.31 a concentrated one.

Collected by rank rather than by sub-type: 49 sherds on rank 1, 153 on rank 2, 244 on rank 3. Cup and plate merge because they share a stage.

\[ N=\sum_j c_j = 446 \qquad \sum_j r_j c_j = 1087 \qquad \sum_j r_j^{2} c_j = 2857 \]
The three sums the SPARQL query returns. Everything after this point is four lines of Python and needs no further access to the graph.

Step 1 — the mean rank.

\[ \overline{x}=\frac{1087}{446}=2.437220 \;\longrightarrow\; \textbf{2.44} \]

Step 2 — the sum of squared deviations, in the computational form that needs only the three sums:

\[ \overline{x}^{\,2}=5.940040 \qquad N\overline{x}^{\,2}=446\times 5.940040=2649.257848 \] \[ \sum_j r_j^{2}c_j - N\overline{x}^{\,2}=2857-2649.257848=207.742152 \]

Step 3 — variance and standard deviation. The denominator is \(N-1\), not \(N\): that is what STDDEV_SAMP means.

\[ \mathrm{Var}=\frac{207.742152}{445}=0.466836 \qquad s=\sqrt{0.466836}=0.683254 \;\longrightarrow\; \textbf{0.68} \]

Step 4 — the bounded score.

\[ \mathrm{CV}=\frac{0.683254}{2.437220}=0.280342 \qquad q=e^{-0.280342}=0.755526 \;\longrightarrow\; \textbf{0.756} \]

Identical to step 2, as it must be. The rank-3 sherds contribute most of the dispersion not because they are far from the mean — rank 1 is three times further — but because there are 244 of them. That is the weighting doing its work, and it is why the same six sub-types in a differently composed horizon give a different \(s\).

Why the computational form is the one in the code. \(\sum c(r-\overline{x})^{2}\) needs \(\overline{x}\) before it can start, so it requires two passes over the data. The form using \(\sum r^{2}c\) needs one, which is what lets the whole cell be described by three numbers a query can aggregate. On 446 sherds the difference is nothing; on the graph it is the difference between a statistic that travels and one that does not.
Rounding. The printed 0.68 and 2.44 are display values. Recomputing from them — \(0.68/2.44=0.2787\), \(e^{-0.2787}=0.7567\) — misses the published \(q\) by a thousandth. The accompanying workbook carries full precision, and any figure quoted in the text should be taken from there rather than read off the plot.

Within, not between. The variance is taken inside each group over its own stage sequence. It is not the variance of the group means against one another, and not a variance of findspots. A cell answers: how far is this group's material in this horizon spread over the group's own stages?

It does not measure how much of a group is present. That is a different question and, for this material, the stronger dating signal: Service II's share of the assemblage runs from about 6 % in horizon 1 to about 92 % in horizon 5. The variance panel is blind to it by design. The two must be read side by side, and the share belongs in the caption so that a flat Service II column is not mistaken for an absent one.

From \(\sigma\) to \(q\)

The right panel. One dimensionless step, and it is smaller than it looks.

\[ \mathrm{CV}_{g,h} = \frac{s_{g,h}}{\overline{r}_{g,h}} \qquad\qquad q_{g,h} = \exp\!\left(-\,\mathrm{CV}_{g,h}\right) \]
The coefficient of variation makes the spread dimensionless by measuring it in units of the group's own mean rank; the exponential maps \([0,\infty)\) onto \((0,1]\), so \(q = 1\) is perfect internal agreement and \(q \to 0\) is scatter far exceeding the mean. This is the same \(\exp(-\mathrm{CV})\) construction as \(q_{\text{interval}}\) in the findspot-dating note — not Pielou's evenness, with which it is easily confused.

Because \(q\) is invertible, the mean rank behind each cell can be read back off the printed pair as \(\overline{x} = s / (-\ln q)\). For the Service I column the true values are 2.44, 2.49, 2.74, 2.77 and 2.89 — the mean creeps up by less than half a rank while \(s\) more than halves, so the rise in \(q\) up the column is overwhelmingly a fall in dispersion, not a shift of the centre.

Why the two panels are one quantity

And why the colour ramps run in opposite directions.

The ramps are mirrored so that green means good in both panels: low dispersion on the left, high quality on the right. That is a deliberate reader aid, and it is the reason the two panels look like independent confirmations when they are not.

The degenerate cell

Ten of the twelve occupied cells. The single most important thing to declare.

Every cell in the Assiette à bord oblique and Service II columns reads \(0.00\) on the left and \(1.00\) on the right, in every horizon, from \(n = 4\) to \(n = 1231\). A reader who takes the right panel at face value concludes that Service II is chronologically impeccable throughout. That is not what the number says.

The cause is now exactly identifiable from §3. Service II holds a single stage: its cup and its plate both carry stage rank 1. Every one of its 3773 sherds is therefore an observation of the value 1, and the standard deviation of a constant is zero whatever \(N\) is. The oblique-rim plate is the same situation more starkly — a group with one leaf. Neither cell is a measurement; both are restatements of the rank table.

\(q = 1.00\) here means "no comparison was possible", not "the comparison came out perfect". A cell whose sherds all carry one rank has no within-group dispersion to measure; the RGZM formula returns its neutral element and the colour scale renders it as the best possible outcome. As a plot this is merely misleading. Exported to RDF as a quality assertion on a temporal entity it is false, and the same COALESCE-style objection applies as to the fabricated fallbacks in the findspot-dating query.
The pipeline already knows. The query returns COUNT(DISTINCT ?stage) and the figure code sets measured = stageSteps > 1, which is precisely what drives the grey fill. The status is computed, not guessed — so the only thing missing is that the printed figure does not say it. Two cheap improvements: put the number of ranks in the cell (k = 1) so grey is self-explanatory on paper as it already is in the browser tooltip, and state in the caption that grey means follows from the rank table rather than low value.
The alternative reading is already implemented. The interactive version of this figure carries a switch between the stage reading and a sub-type reading in which cup and plate count as separate steps (columnRank). Under that reading Service II becomes measured — \(q\) about 0.71 in every horizon, flat, because the cup–plate ratio barely moves. That flatness is itself the argument for the stage reading: the variation it picks up is a change of vessel form, not a step in time. For Service I the two readings order the horizons identically, with \(r = 0.998\) between their \(q\) values, so the published result does not depend on the choice.

What \(n\) is

The sample size. It is the weighting, and it is already applied.

Correction to an earlier draft of this page. A previous version stated that \(n\) is printed but does not enter the calculation, and asked whether the ranks should be weighted by sherd count. That was a misreading of the figure and is withdrawn. The measure is sherd-weighted already: rgzm() takes \(N\), \(\sum r\cdot c\) and \(\sum r^{2}c\), so every sherd is one observation valued by the rank of its sub-type. Nothing needs changing. What follows is the evidence, and what the unweighted alternative would have cost.

So \(n\) is not an annotation beside the statistic — it is the \(N\) inside it. Horizon 1 of Service I is not four or six observations but 446, distributed 49 on rank 1, 153 on rank 2 and 244 on rank 3. The sums the SPARQL query returns are exactly the sufficient statistics for that weighting, which is why the query can stop at three numbers and leave the square root and the exponential to Python.

\[ N = \sum_j c_j \qquad \overline{x} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_j r_j c_j \qquad s = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_j r_j^{2} c_j - N\overline{x}^{2}}{N-1}} \]
\(c_j\) is the sherd count of sub-type \(j\), \(r_j\) its stage rank. Written this way the weighting is visible; written as STDDEV_SAMP(rank) over a table of sherds it is the same thing and easier to misread, which is what happened here.

What the unweighted alternative would do

Giving each sub-type one vote instead of one vote per sherd is a coherent measure — it describes the typological repertoire rather than the assemblage. On this material it is also useless, and the reason is worth stating because it is not obvious in advance.

The two figures do agree. The findspot-dating method weights by occurrence; this one weights by sherd count. Both let the quantity of material speak, and the earlier claim that they diverge was wrong. The remaining difference is one of unit — occurrences of a stamp there, sherds of a sub-type here — and that is a difference in what is being counted, not in how it is weighted.

Reading the Service I column

The only column that carries a signal, and what that signal is.

Service I is the sole group whose sub-types straddle stages in every horizon, so it is the only column in which the measurement is defined throughout. Its dispersion falls monotonically from horizon 1 to horizon 5.

Which of the two to lead with. \(s\) is in rank units and independent of the rank origin; \(q\) is bounded, comparable across analyses, and depends on the numbering declared in §11a. For a statement about this material — horizon 5 is tighter than horizon 1 — either will do. For a number quoted beside results from elsewhere, \(q\) is the one that travels, provided the declaration travels with it.
Two readings, both consistent with the numbers. Either the later horizons genuinely draw on a narrower typological repertoire, or they are small enough that rarer sub-types fail to appear. A rarefaction check would separate them; it is not part of the present analysis, so the honest form in the text is to state the gradient together with the sample sizes and leave the two readings standing. The gradient is monotone across a seventeen-fold range of \(n\), which is not what a pure sample-size artefact usually looks like, but it is not a substitute for the test.

Limitations and open questions

To be settled before the figure goes into the paper.

  1. Ranks by stage only. Because sub-types of one stage share a rank, the dispersion of any single-stage group is zero by construction. This is the direct cause of the two flat columns. It is the right modelling decision on the archaeology — a cup and a plate of one stage are contemporary — but it means those columns carry no measurement and the caption must say so. §3
  2. Undefined dispersion printed as a number. Where a group holds one rank, \(s = 0\) and \(q = 1\) follow from the rank table, not from the sherds. The figure greys them; the greying is computed from COUNT(DISTINCT ?stage) and is therefore reliable, but it is not labelled. §7
  3. Within-group spread is not group share. The panels say nothing about how much of each group is present, which for this material is the stronger signal (Service II: about 6 % to about 92 % across the sequence). Report the two together. §4
  4. Dataset-relative colour scaling on the left panel. The variance ramp ends at the largest observed value, so colours are not stable across runs. §6
  5. Confounding of \(s\) with sample size. Dispersion and \(n\) fall together along the Service I column: horizon 5 rests on 103 sherds against horizon 2's 1741. A rarefaction check would separate them. §9
  6. Horizon assignment is a hardcoded convention. Five classes from FINDSPOT_HORIZON, with a label bridge for findspots the workbook and the TTL spell differently. Maintainable and single-sourced, but it must travel with the figure as provenance rather than living only in the module.
  7. Ordinal ranks under a ratio-scale statistic — settled by declaration. The coefficient of variation divides by the mean, so \(q\) depends on where the rank numbering starts, while \(s\) does not. The project needs \(q\), so the numbering is now part of the method rather than an implementation detail: the first stage of every group carries rank 1, and ranks run 1…k in stage order with no gaps. It is derived from TYPES in services_to_rdf.py and published as aeont:stageRank, so any reader can recompute against it. See below for what is being fixed. §11 a
Decision: the rank origin is fixed at 1 and declared. \(q\) is needed, so the alternative — dropping \(q\) and reporting \(s\) alone — was not taken. What follows from that is an obligation rather than a caveat: the numbering must be stated in the method section, carried in the graph as aeont:stageRank, and repeated in the figure caption, because a \(q\) quoted without it cannot be reproduced.

What the declaration fixes

How much of \(q\) is the data and how much is the numbering.

What this licenses in the text. Comparisons between horizons — "horizon 5 is tighter than horizon 1" — are safe: they survive any numbering. A bare quoted level — "the quality of horizon 3 is 0.85" — is only meaningful together with the declaration, and is not comparable with a \(q\) from any other study unless that study numbers its ranks the same way. The safest formulation in the paper is to lead with \(s\), which is origin-free, and to introduce \(q\) as its bounded presentation.
Three places the numbering has to appear. The method section, as one sentence; the graph, where aeont:stageRank already carries it per concept, so the assertion is machine-checkable; and the figure caption, since the figure will be reused without the paper around it. If it appears in only the first two, the caption is the one that will be quoted.
